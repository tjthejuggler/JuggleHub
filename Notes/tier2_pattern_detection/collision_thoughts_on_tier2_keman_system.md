This is a fantastic and advanced use case. The ability to analyze intentional collisions is a true test of a robust tracking and analysis system.

The great news is that the architecture we've designed is not only capable of handling this, but it's almost *perfectly* suited for it. The "glass-box" analytical approach is exactly what's needed. We don't need to change the core architecture; we just need to extend its "vocabulary."

Here's exactly how it works into our current implementation.

### The Key Insight: A Collision is Just Another Event

Our system is built on detecting "events" by looking for moments when a ball deviates from its predictable ballistic path. We've defined two causes for this deviation so far: a `THROW` and a `CATCH`.

An intentional mid-air collision is simply a third type of event. It's a moment when a ball's trajectory changes suddenly and dramatically due to a force that is **not gravity and not a hand.**

The Kalman filter is the key. When two balls collide, the filter's prediction for each ball (to continue on its ballistic path) will be massively different from the measurement (the ball suddenly changing direction and velocity). This will create a huge **innovation spike** for *both balls at the same time and in the same location*.

This gives us a unique and detectable "collision signature."

### The Implementation: A Collision Detection Module

This logic would be a new component within the Python hub's analysis pipeline, running alongside the throw/catch detector in Tier 1.

#### Step 1: Detecting the Collision Signature (in Tier 1)

The event detection logic in Tier 1 becomes more sophisticated. Instead of just looking for any innovation spike, it now classifies the *cause* of the spike.

The logic would be:

1.  The system detects a large innovation spike for `Ball_A` at `time_T` and `location_X`.
2.  It immediately checks if any other balls also had a large innovation spike at the same time and location. It finds that `Ball_B` also has a spike at `time_T` and `location_X`.
3.  It then checks for the proximity of hands. Is `Hand_Left` or `Hand_Right` near `location_X`?
    *   **If YES:** This is a complex hand interaction (like a grab or a mid-air catch/throw).
    *   **If NO:** The system concludes this was a non-hand interaction. The only physical possibility is a collision between `Ball_A` and `Ball_B`.
4.  The system generates a new event type: `JugglingEvent(type=COLLISION, ball_ids=[A, B], timestamp=T, location=X)`.

#### Step 2: Correcting the Tracker (The Hardest Part)

A major challenge with collisions is that object trackers like ByteTrack will almost certainly get confused. When two balls merge and re-emerge, the tracker is very likely to **swap their identities**. `Ball_A` goes in, but the tracker follows it out as `Ball_B`.

Our new Collision Detection Module can solve this. Because it knows a collision just occurred between `Ball_A` and `Ball_B`, it can enforce a logic rule *after* the event:

*   "I have just detected a collision between the tracks for `Ball_A` and `Ball_B`."
*   "In the next few frames, I will look for two balls emerging from the collision point."
*   "Regardless of what the tracker calls them, I will re-label them to ensure the identities of `Ball_A` and `Ball_B` are preserved." (This can be done intelligently by looking at the post-collision velocity vectors).

This makes your system far more robust than a standard object tracker alone. It uses a higher-level physical understanding to correct the lower-level perception.

#### Step 3: Describing the Pattern (in Tier 2)

Now that we have a new, reliable `COLLISION` event, we can integrate it into our Tier 2 Pattern Descriptor.

Let's imagine a simple 3-ball pattern where two balls are thrown up to collide in the middle, and the third ball is passed underneath.

The Pattern Descriptor generated by Tier 2 would look something like this:

```json
{
  "pattern_name": "Unclassified Collision Pattern",
  "siteswap": "N/A (Collision Interrupts Sequence)",
  "properties": {
    "tempo_bpm": 110,
    "consistency": 0.85,
    "events_per_cycle": [
      "THROW(hand=L, ball=A)",
      "THROW(hand=R, ball=B)",
      "PASS(hand=L, ball=C)",
      "COLLISION(balls=[A, B])",
      "CATCH(hand=R, ball=A)",
      "CATCH(hand=L, ball=B)",
      "CATCH(hand=R, ball=C)"
    ]
  }
}
```

As you can see, the system doesn't need a traditional Siteswap. It describes the pattern as a repeating sequence of its fundamental event "atoms," and `COLLISION` is now one of those atoms. You can then save this descriptor and give it a name, like "3-Ball Center Collision," and the system will be able to recognize it perfectly in the future.

### Getting Stats (Your Goal)

This architecture makes getting detailed statistics trivial:

*   **Collision Count:** The system can simply count the number of `COLLISION` events per session.
*   **Collision Accuracy:** For each collision, you can measure the `location_X`. Over time, you can calculate the mean and standard deviation of your collision point to measure your consistency. Are you always colliding at the center, or is your impact point drifting?
*   **Post-Collision Analysis:** You can analyze the trajectories of the balls *after* the collision. Are they flying off cleanly and predictably? The system can measure this and give you a "post-collision stability" score.
*   **Timing:** Is the collision happening at the rhythmic "beat" you intend? The system can measure this timing relative to your throws.

The current `VISION.md` is robust enough to encompass this without a major rewrite. The concept of "Throw Atomization" in Tier 2 can be interpreted to include collision events.
